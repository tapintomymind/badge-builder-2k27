== Why the contrast spread is not flat ==
Each hue sits where it still READS as its named colour at full saturation,
not at a shared target. A saturated red cannot reach 6.6:1 on this canvas
without turning pink — which is exactly why --danger ships at 5.65:1 — and
a yellow cannot go below ~8:1 without turning olive. An earlier cut forced
one register and produced a salmon Defense and a teal Shooting, which fails
the actual requirement. The spread is bounded (<3.5) and asserted, not
eliminated.

== The collisions that remain, and why they are safe ==
Nearest state colour is --info for Finishing (ΔE 7) — both blues. Nearest
level colours are --lvl-legend/Rebounding (ΔE 11), --lvl-gold/Playmaking
(ΔE 12), --lvl-hof/Physicals (ΔE 17).

None of these pairs ever share a surface. §2.8.1 confines the identity
layer to four places — attribute slider fill, attribute group legend,
category digest title, jump-nav chip border — and bars it from every
state-bearing surface. Two are load-bearing and pinned by test:
  · .category-ledger's left border, which --danger takes on overspend. A
    red Defense identity there would be pixel-identical to "over budget".
  · the ledger/summary numerals on §2.7.4's forbidden list.
The level palette lives on pips and medallions INSIDE cards; the identity
layer lives on the chrome around them.

== Colour-vision deficiency ==
The simulated worst pairs above are real and are NOT designed away: a
six-hue palette containing both red and green cannot be separated by hue
under deuteranopia, and a red/magenta pair cannot under tritanopia.
§6 is the mitigation, and it is structural rather than a promise — all
four consuming surfaces render the category NAME as text:
  slider fill      inside a <fieldset> whose <legend> is the group name
  group legend     IS the name
  digest title     IS the name
  jump-nav chip    label stays --fg-secondary, never the category colour,
                   so chip legibility never depends on the palette at all
Colour is reinforcement on every one of them. Removing all six colours
loses no information, which is the test §6 actually asks.
